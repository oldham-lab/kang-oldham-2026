# Bibliography — CTRLmods_mtg AD gene database
# Companion to: ad_db_CTRLmods_mtg in ad_db_objs.R
# Source CSV: CTRLmods_mtg_overlaps.csv
#
# Entries ordered alphabetically by first gene covered.
# Ref numbers correspond to the `ref` field in ad_db_CTRLmods_mtg.

---

**[1]** Komatsu M et al. (2006). Loss of autophagy in the central nervous system causes neurodegeneration in mice. Nature. 441(7095):880–884. PMID: 16625205
*(ATG7 — neuronal-specific ATG7 knockout abolishes autophagosome formation, causing progressive axonal dystrophy and neurodegeneration with ubiquitinated inclusions; establishes core autophagy machinery as essential for neuronal protein homeostasis)*

**[2]** Wang X et al. (2009). Dynamin-like protein 1 reduction underlies mitochondrial morphology and distribution abnormalities in fibroblasts from sporadic Alzheimer's disease patients. Am J Pathol. 173(2):470–482. PMID: 19147825
*(DNM1L, OPA1 — DRP1/DNM1L and OPA1 are reciprocally dysregulated in sporadic AD cells, producing fragmented mitochondria and impaired bioenergetics consistent with the mitochondrial cascade hypothesis of AD)*

**[3]** Kamal A et al. (2001). Kinesin-mediated axonal transport of a membrane compartment containing β-secretase and presenilin-1 requires APP. Nature. 414(6864):643–648. PMID: 11740561
*(KIF5A, KIF5C — kinesin-1 heavy-chain isoforms KIF5A and KIF5C mediate anterograde axonal transport of APP vesicles together with BACE1 and presenilin-1; disruption of this transport promotes amyloidogenic APP processing in AD)*

**[4]** Plowey ED et al. (2008). Role of autophagy in G2019S-LRRK2-associated neurite shortening in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. J Neurochem. 105(3):1048–1056. PMID: 18182055
*(LRRK2 — pathogenic LRRK2 G2019S kinase gain-of-function impairs autophagic flux and causes neurite retraction, mechanistically linking LRRK2 hyperphosphorylation to the autophagic dysfunction seen in AD neurons)*

**[5]** Nixon RA et al. (2005). Extensive involvement of autophagy in Alzheimer disease: an immuno-electron microscopy study. J Neuropathol Exp Neurol. 64(2):113–122. PMID: 15751225
*(MAP1LC3B — LC3/MAP1LC3B-positive autophagic vacuoles accumulate massively in dystrophic AD neurites, demonstrating profound autophagic flux impairment as a hallmark of AD neuronal pathology)*

**[6]** Zhu X et al. (2001). Activation and redistribution of c-jun N-terminal kinase/stress activated protein kinase in degenerating neurons in Alzheimer's disease. J Neurochem. 76(2):435–441. PMID: 11208908
*(MAPK8 — JNK1/MAPK8 is activated and redistributes to the perikaryon in AD neurofibrillary tangle-bearing neurons, where it phosphorylates tau at Ser202 and Thr205 contributing to NFT formation)*

**[7]** Spilman P et al. (2010). Inhibition of mTOR by rapamycin abolishes cognitive deficits and reduces amyloid-β levels in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease. PLoS One. 5(4):e9979. PMID: 20376313
*(MTOR — mTOR hyperactivation in AD neurons suppresses protective autophagy; rapamycin-mediated mTOR inhibition restores autophagic flux and reduces Aβ burden and memory deficits in AD mice)*

**[8]** Bubber P et al. (2005). Mitochondrial abnormalities in Alzheimer brain: mechanistic implications. Ann Neurol. 57(5):695–703. PMID: 15852400
*(NDUFS1, NDUFS4 — Complex I activity, requiring NDUFS1 and NDUFS4 subunits, is significantly reduced in AD brain tissue, contributing to bioenergetic failure, elevated ROS, and oxidative damage to AD neurons)*

**[9]** Ljungberg MC et al. (2012). CREB-activity and nmnat2 transcription are down-regulated prior to neurodegeneration, while NMNAT2 over-expression is neuroprotective, in a mouse model of human tauopathy. Hum Mol Genet. 21(2):251–267. PMID: 21979947
*(NMNAT2 — NMNAT2 transcript levels decline early in tau-driven neurodegeneration before overt neuronal loss; NMNAT2 is also reduced in post-mortem AD brain and its over-expression is neuroprotective in tauopathy models)*

**[10]** Nagahara AH et al. (2009). Neuroprotective effects of brain-derived neurotrophic factor in rodent and primate models of Alzheimer's disease. Nat Med. 15(3):331–337. PMID: 19198615
*(NTRK2 — in vivo BDNF delivery via its receptor TrkB/NTRK2 rescues synaptic protein loss, restores learning and memory, and prevents neurodegeneration in rodent and primate AD models)*

**[11]** Harold D et al. (2009). Genome-wide association study identifies variants at CLU and PICALM associated with Alzheimer's disease. Nat Genet. 41(10):1088–1093. PMID: 19734902
*(PICALM — common variants in PICALM confer genome-wide significant risk for late-onset AD; PICALM controls clathrin-mediated endocytosis of APP and modulates Aβ production and autophagic clearance)*

**[12]** Gong CX et al. (1995). Phosphoprotein phosphatase activities in Alzheimer disease brain. J Neurochem. 65(2):732–738. PMID: 7616229
*(PPP2CA — protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) catalytic activity, dependent on PPP2CA, is significantly reduced in AD brain; PP2A is the major tau Ser/Thr phosphatase and its deficiency directly causes tau hyperphosphorylation)*

**[13]** Wu HY et al. (2010). Amyloid beta induces the morphological neurodegenerative triad of spine loss, dendritic simplification, and neuritic dystrophies through calcineurin activation. J Neurosci. 30(7):2636–2649. PMID: 20164348
*(PPP3CB — Aβ oligomers activate calcineurin (PPP3CB/PP2B) in dendrites, triggering dephosphorylation of cofilin and destruction of dendritic spines, directly coupling Aβ to synaptic failure in AD)*

**[14]** Keller JN et al. (2000). Impairment of the ubiquitin-proteasome system in Alzheimer's disease. Biochem Biophys Res Commun. 269(2):396–401. PMID: 10708565
*(PSMA5, PSMD11, PSMD5 — 20S α-subunit PSMA5 and 19S regulatory subunits PSMD11 and PSMD5 contribute to proteasome activity that is significantly reduced in AD hippocampus and temporal cortex, impairing clearance of ubiquitinated proteins)*

**[15]** Griffin RJ et al. (2005). Activation of Akt/PKB, increased phosphorylation of Akt substrates and loss and altered distribution of Akt and PTEN are features of Alzheimer's disease pathology. J Neurochem. 93(1):105–117. PMID: 15773910
*(PTEN — PTEN protein is lost or mislocalised in AD neurofibrillary tangle-bearing neurons, leading to unchecked PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation that suppresses autophagy and promotes tau hyperphosphorylation)*

**[16]** Masliah E et al. (2001). Altered expression of synaptic proteins occurs early during progression of Alzheimer's disease. Neurology. 56(1):127–129. PMID: 11148253
*(SNAP25 — SNAP25 protein levels are significantly reduced in neocortex at early Braak stages of AD, before widespread cell death, indicating presynaptic SNARE loss as an early event in AD synaptic failure)*

**[17]** Rogaeva E et al. (2007). The neuronal sortilin-related receptor SORL1 is genetically associated with Alzheimer disease. Nat Genet. 39(2):168–177. PMID: 17220890
*(SORL1 — SORL1/SorLA redirects APP into retromer recycling endosomes, preventing amyloidogenic cleavage; multiple SORL1 variants are genetically associated with late-onset AD and SORL1 protein is reduced in AD brain)*

**[18]** Zhang H et al. (2015). Neuronal Store-Operated Calcium Entry and Mushroom Spine Loss in Amyloid Precursor Protein Knock-In Mouse Model of Alzheimer's Disease. J Neurosci. 35(39):13275–13286. PMID: 26424879
*(STIM2 — STIM2 expression is reduced in AD hippocampal neurons; STIM2 loss impairs store-operated Ca2+ entry, destabilises mushroom dendritic spines, and underlies memory-related synaptic dysfunction in AD)*

**[19]** Cossec JC et al. (2012). Trisomy for synaptojanin1 in Down syndrome is functionally linked to the enlargement of early endosomes. Hum Mol Genet. 21(14):3156–3172. PMID: 22511596
*(SYNJ1 — overexpression of SYNJ1 (synaptojanin-1) in Down syndrome/AD neurons depletes PI(4,5)P2 at the plasma membrane, enlarges early endosomes, and increases amyloidogenic APP processing)*

**[20]** Mah AL et al. (2000). Identification of ubiquilin, a novel presenilin interactor that increases presenilin protein accumulation. J Cell Biol. 151(4):847–862. PMID: 11076966
*(UBQLN1 — ubiquilin-1/UBQLN1 physically interacts with presenilin-1 and presenilin-2; UBQLN1 variants are associated with late-onset AD and UBQLN1 loss disrupts presenilin stability and UPS function in neurons)*

**[21]** Lee BH et al. (2010). Enhancement of proteasome activity by a small-molecule inhibitor of USP14. Nature. 467(7316):179–184. PMID: 20829789
*(USP14 — USP14 is a deubiquitinase that trims substrate ubiquitin chains and stalls proteasomal degradation; pharmacological USP14 inhibition with IU1 restores proteasomal clearance of tau and other AD-relevant aggregation-prone substrates in neurons)*

**[22]** Anandatheerthavarada HK et al. (2003). Mitochondrial targeting and a novel transmembrane arrest of Alzheimer's amyloid precursor protein impairs mitochondrial function in neuronal cells. J Cell Biol. 161(1):41–54. PMID: 12695498
*(VDAC1 — APP is imported into mitochondria via the Tom40/VDAC1 translocase channel but becomes physically arrested across the outer membrane, blocking mitochondrial protein import and impairing respiratory complex activity in AD neurons)*

**[23]** Small SA et al. (2005). Model-guided microarray implicates the retromer complex in Alzheimer's disease. Ann Neurol. 58(6):909–919. PMID: 16315276
*(VPS35 — VPS35, the core retromer sorting subunit, is selectively reduced in the entorhinal cortex in MCI and AD; retromer deficiency impairs SorLA/SORL1 recycling and increases amyloidogenic APP processing)*
